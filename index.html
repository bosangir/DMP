What you should be able to do after reading this page.

What a Data Management Plan is and why it is called a living document.

The stages of the research data lifecycle and which DMP section belongs to each stage.

Recognise

The DMP stages that FWO, BOF, BELSPO and Horizon Europe ask for, and their deadlines.

Start

A plan in DMPonline.be, so that your group can work on its DMP project from the first session.

Why this matters for your grade

Half of this course is a group project in which you write a Data Management Plan for a study, in groups of about five. A good DMP is specific: it names systems, people and numbers, justifies each choice, and links those choices to FAIR and the GDPR. Keep this in mind as you read.

02What is a Data Management Plan?

A written plan for your data before, during and after the project.

The University of Antwerp describes a DMP as a formal document, required by most research funders, that covers three things: the data a project will generate or reuse, how those data will be handled during the project, and how they will be preserved and shared afterwards.[1] KU Leuven adds that a DMP works as an RDM checklist for your project, and that by the end of the project it should record what data were produced, where they are stored, how they can be accessed and how they may be reused.[7]

The Digital Curation Centre (DCC) in the UK, whose software DMPonline.be is built on, uses a slightly wider term: a data management and sharing plan. In its words, such a plan explains how data will be created, managed, shared and preserved, and justifies any restrictions on access.[11]

① Before

Which data will you collect or reuse? In what format and volume? Is ethics approval or a DPIA needed?

② During

Where are the data stored and backed up? Who has access? How are files named, versioned and documented?

③ After

What will you keep, for how long, and where? What can be shared, with whom, and under which licence?

A living document

Every source used here makes the same point: a DMP is not written once and filed away. UHasselt calls it "a living document" that records your intentions and is updated as the project runs.[4] UAntwerp asks you to update it regularly, based on what you learn during the project.[1] In practice, plans change: a new data source is added, the ethics committee asks for extra protection, or a partner wants a data-sharing agreement. The DMP should follow those changes.

A DMP is not a place to store data

UHasselt's guidance says this directly: the DMP describes your data and how you handle them; it does not contain the data themselves.[4] Never paste participant data, names or identifiers into a DMP.

03Why write one?

Funders require it, but the main benefit is to you and your team.

UAntwerp lists three reasons to plan your data management:[1]

  1. Know the requirements. Funders, journals, the university and the law (for example the GDPR) all have rules about data. Planning shows you early whether you need ethics clearance, a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA), contracts with partners or a patent check.
  2. Plan the work. Thinking ahead about file names, versions, README files, metadata, repositories and licences saves time later. It also protects the project if a team member leaves.
  3. Get more out of your research. Well-documented, shared data can be cited, reused and built on.

The DCC adds that planning reduces duplicated effort, makes it easier for others to validate your published results, and shows funders that your project fits their data policies.[11] UHasselt notes that it lowers the risk of losing data or mixing up versions.[4]

An epidemiology example

Imagine a tuberculosis cohort in which the data manager leaves halfway through follow-up. Without a plan, nobody knows which spreadsheet is the current one, where the pseudonymisation key is kept, or which variables were recoded. A DMP that names a storage location, a folder structure, a codebook and a backup person avoids exactly this situation.

Data management also costs money: storage beyond what the university provides, licensed software, encryption tools or a data manager's time. UAntwerp treats these as normal research costs that belong in the project budget, and points to the UK Data Service costing tool to estimate them.[1]

04The research data lifecycle

Data pass through stages. The DMP is how you plan each one.

Research data management is usually described as a cycle. Different institutions draw it slightly differently, but the stages are much the same. The UAntwerp RDM pages, for example, are organised as collecting, processing, storage and backup, preservation, sharing, licensing and reuse.[2] KU Leuven's guidance follows the same path, from documentation and storage during the project to preservation and sharing at the end.[6] In this course we use six stages, with two things that run through all of them.

StageTypical questionsWhere it goes in your DMP
1 · PlanWhat data, how much, from where? Who is responsible? What will it cost? Do I need ethics approval or a DPIA?All sections; especially Responsibilities & resources and Legal & ethical
2 · CollectWhich tool (REDCap, KoboToolbox, paper CRF)? Which formats? Does the consent form cover storage, sharing and reuse?Data description & collection
3 · ProcessHow are raw data protected from editing? How is cleaning documented? Which codebook, metadata standard, quality checks?Documentation & data quality; Storage & backup
4 · PreserveWhich data are kept, in which open formats, for how long, and in which archive?Sharing & long-term preservation
5 · ShareWhich repository? Open, restricted or closed access? Which licence? Is a data-sharing agreement needed?Sharing & long-term preservation; Legal & ethical
6 · ReuseCan someone else understand and cite the data? Are they findable with a persistent identifier (DOI)?Documentation; Sharing

Documentation (README files, codebooks, metadata) and security (access rights, encryption, backups) are not one-off steps. They apply from the first questionnaire to the final deposit, which is why they sit in the centre of the diagram.

05The DMP lifecycle

A DMP has versions, and each one has a deadline and an audience.

Not only the data have a lifecycle; the plan does too. For most Flemish funders the DMP passes through four versions.[3][4][9]

1

Application DMP

Part of the grant proposal. For FWO this is a short section of the application form with six questions about data types, preservation for at least five years, reasons to deviate, ethics and security, other issues, and who might reuse the data. Several questions have a 700-character limit, and the evaluation panel and external referees read the answers.[9]

2

Initial full DMP

Written at the start of the funded project using the full template. For FWO projects, it is due within six months of the official start date and goes to the research coordination office of the main host institution, not to FWO itself; FWO may ask for it in a random check.[9] At UAntwerp you send it to the RDM team, who give feedback and archive it.[3]

3

Updates during the project

Revise the plan whenever your practice changes, for example after advice from the ethics committee or a discussion with the technology transfer office.[7] UHasselt notes that you do not need to send these intermediate updates to the RDM team.[4]

4

Final DMP

At the end, the DMP should describe what was actually done. For FWO it is attached to the final report and submitted through the FWO e-portal; without a detailed DMP the final report is considered incomplete.[9]

07What goes in a DMP

Templates differ in wording, but they ask about the same six things.

In 2019 Science Europe, an association of European research funders, published a set of core requirements for DMPs so that funders would ask broadly the same questions.[12] The Flemish template, the Horizon Europe template and the DCC's guidance all cover the same ground.[7][11] Below are the six sections, with the questions each one must answer and a short example from an epidemiological study.

1

Data description and collection (or reuse of existing data)

What data will you collect, generate or reuse? Which types, formats and volume? How will you collect them, and how do they relate to existing data?

Example
Questionnaire data from about 600 adults with TB, collected in REDCap (≈ 50 MB, exported as CSV); sputum smear and GeneXpert results from the hospital laboratory; routine notification data reused from the national TB programme.
2

Documentation and data quality

Which metadata, codebook and README will accompany the data? Which standards (for example DDI, CDISC, ICD-11, LOINC)? How will you control quality during collection and cleaning?

Example
The REDCap data dictionary serves as the codebook. Range checks and required fields in REDCap; cleaning done only by script in R, with a cleaning log; raw export kept read-only.
3

Storage and backup during the project

Where are data stored and backed up? Who has access, and how is access controlled? How are data kept secure, especially personal data?

Example
Data on the university's secure network drive with automatic backup; access limited to four named team members; pseudonymisation key stored separately and encrypted; no data on personal laptops, USB sticks or email.
4

Legal and ethical requirements, codes of conduct

Are personal data processed under the GDPR? Is there informed consent for storage, sharing and reuse? Ethics approval? A DPIA? Who owns the data, and are agreements needed with partners?

Example
Health data are a special category under GDPR Art. 9. Ethics approval from the institutional and local committees; DPIA completed with the privacy office; data transfer agreement with the partner site outside the EU.
5

Data sharing and long-term preservation

Which data will be kept, for how long, and where? Which data can be shared, through which repository, under which conditions and licence? Will they get a persistent identifier?

Example
De-identified analysis dataset, codebook and R scripts deposited in Zenodo with a DOI under restricted access (request to the PI), kept for at least 10 years. Identifiable source data stay in the university archive and are not shared.
6

Data management responsibilities and resources

Who does what: collection, cleaning, backup, approval, deposit? Who takes over if someone leaves? What will data management cost in money and time?

Example
PI is accountable; the study data manager runs REDCap and the weekly checks; the PhD student deposits the data at the end. Budget: €1,500 for a data manager's time and €0 for storage (institutional).

How the Flemish and Horizon Europe templates map to these sections

Flemish standard template (FWO, BOF, VLAIO): data description; ethical, privacy and legal issues; documentation and metadata; storage, backup and versioning; archiving and preservation; data sharing; responsibilities.[7]

Horizon Europe: data summary; making data FAIR; allocation of resources; data security; ethics.[7][15]

08FAIR, open and GDPR

Three ideas that most DMP questions come back to.

The FAIR principles

The FAIR principles were published in 2016 and are now built into most funder policies.[13] They describe data that are:

F · Findable

A persistent identifier (such as a DOI) and rich metadata, registered in a searchable resource.

A · Accessible

Retrievable by a standard protocol, with clear access conditions. Metadata stay available even if the data are no longer.

I · Interoperable

Open formats and shared vocabularies, so that data can be combined with other data.

R · Reusable

A clear licence, provenance and documentation that meets the standards of the field.

FAIR is not the same as open

Health data are often too sensitive to publish openly, but they can still be FAIR: the metadata can be public and findable, with access to the data granted on request under an agreement. Horizon Europe summarises this as data that are "as open as possible, as closed as necessary".[15]

Personal data and the GDPR

In epidemiology, most DMPs involve personal data. UAntwerp asks you to describe which personal or sensitive data you collect, how you pseudonymise them and where the key is kept, how you secure and how long you keep them, and whether the informed consent covers access, preservation and sharing. It advises a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) at the start of the project.[1] Remember that pseudonymised data are still personal data under the GDPR; only truly anonymised data fall outside it.

Two other legal points can apply to your study. If you work with genetic resources from another country, the Nagoya Protocol on access and benefit-sharing may apply.[1] If the project produces results that could be patented or commercialised, talk to the valorisation office before you share data.

09Using DMPonline.be

The Belgian tool for writing, sharing and exporting DMPs.

DMPonline.be is the Belgian instance of DMPonline, the tool developed by the Digital Curation Centre. It is run by Belnet and used by most Belgian universities, university colleges and research institutes, including UAntwerp, UHasselt, KU Leuven, UGent and UCLouvain.[16] The DMPbelgium consortium that set it up as a shared tool was founded in January 2017 by Ghent University, INBO, the University of Antwerp, Hasselt University, ULB, VUB and Sciensano.[17]

The tool contains the templates of the main funders, guidance under every question (both from the funder and from your university), and options to share a plan with co-authors, request feedback and export to PDF or Word.[4][16]

Step by step

  1. Log in at dmponline.be. Choose your institution (University of Antwerp) and sign in with your university account. You can also sign in with ORCID.[16]
  2. Create a plan. Under Create plans, give a project title, select your organisation and select the funder. If there is no funder, or for BOF and practice plans, tick the no funder option to get the Flemish template.[4]
  3. Check the guidance. Make sure your institution's guidance is ticked, so that its advice appears next to each question.[4]
  4. Choose the right stage. Templates have separate tabs, such as Application DMP and Full DMP. Answer the one that fits the stage of your project.[4]
  5. Write together. Use the Share tab to invite your group members as co-owners or editors.[4]
  6. Request feedback from your institution's RDM team, if the option is available.[4]
  7. Download the plan as PDF or DOCX, and keep updating it as the project develops.[4]

A user manual for DMPonline.be is maintained by the DMPbelgium consortium on GitHub.[18] KU Leuven reports that a new version of DMPonline.be released in September 2026 includes a validation tab; follow your own institution's instructions on whether to use it.[7]

You don't have to use DMPonline.be, but it helps

UHasselt and FWO both accept a plan written in the Word version of the template, as long as all questions of the funder's template are answered.[4][9] For this course we will use DMPonline.be, because it shows the guidance next to each question and makes group work easier.

11Writing a good DMP

What separates a useful plan from a box-ticking exercise.

Be specific

Name the system, the folder, the person and the number. "Data will be stored securely" says nothing; "on the UAntwerp network drive with daily backup, access for 4 named people" says a lot.

Justify your choices

Funders rarely prescribe formats, repositories or access levels. Explain why yours fit your study.[11]

Start early, consult early

Talk to the RDM team, IT, the privacy office, the ethics committee and your target repository before you finalise the plan.[11]

Use what exists

Point to institutional storage, existing SOPs and established repositories instead of inventing your own.[11]

Name the people

Give each task an owner and a backup. Say who takes over if someone leaves.[11]

Keep it alive

Version number and date on the plan; review it at each milestone and when something changes.[1][7]

Common mistakes

  • Copying generic text from the guidance or from an example plan without adapting it to the study.
  • Leaving questions empty. If a question does not apply, write "not applicable" and say why.
  • Saying data will be "anonymised" when they are only pseudonymised.
  • Promising open data for sensitive health data without checking consent, the GDPR and partner agreements.
  • Forgetting the costs of data management, or the person responsible for it.
  • Writing the plan once and never updating it.

Check your plan the way a reviewer would

KU Leuven publishes a DMP self-evaluation rubric,[7] and the extended Science Europe guide includes a rubric for reviewers.[12] Use one of them on your group's draft. Real examples of Horizon Europe DMPs can be found on CORDIS.[7]

13Check yourself

A random practice quiz first, then a few open questions.

Practice quiz: 20 random questions

Each round draws 20 questions at random from a bank of 36, in a different order each time. If you get one wrong, you see the right answer and why straight away. Score 14 or more (70%) to pass.

Question 1 of 20Score: 0

Open questions

Try to answer first, then open the answer.

1. Name the three things every DMP describes.

The data you will collect or reuse; how you will handle them during the project; and how you will preserve and share them afterwards.[1]

2. Why is a DMP called a "living document"?

Because it is updated as the project develops. The first version states your intentions; the final version should describe what was actually done.[4][7]

3. You receive an FWO project starting on 1 January. When is the full DMP due, and to whom?

Within six months of the official start date, so by 30 June. It goes to the research coordination office of the main host institution (at UAntwerp, the RDM team), not to FWO. The final DMP goes to FWO with the final report.[9][3]

4. Your dataset is pseudonymised. Does the GDPR still apply?

Yes. Pseudonymised data can be linked back to a person with the key, so they are still personal data. Only data that are irreversibly anonymised fall outside the GDPR.

5. Can sensitive health data be FAIR without being open?

Yes. The metadata can be public and findable with a DOI, while access to the data themselves is restricted and granted under conditions: "as open as possible, as closed as necessary".[13][15]

6. Which DMP section would cover a data transfer agreement with a partner in Uganda?

Legal and ethical requirements (agreements, GDPR rules on transfers outside the EU, local data protection law), with a link to the sharing section if the partner will receive data.

7. What is DMPonline.be, and who runs it?

The Belgian online tool for writing, sharing and exporting DMPs, based on the DCC's DMPonline. It is run by Belnet for the DMPbelgium consortium of institutions.[16][17]

14Glossary

Terms you will meet in templates and guidance.

RDM
Research data management: everything you do to keep research data usable, safe and trustworthy, from planning to reuse.
DMP
Data Management Plan: a written, versioned plan describing how data will be handled during and after a project.
Metadata
Data about data: who created them, when, how, what the variables mean, and under which licence they can be used.
Codebook / data dictionary
A list of all variables with their names, labels, types, units and allowed values.
Persistent identifier (PID)
A permanent reference to a dataset or person, such as a DOI for data or an ORCID iD for researchers.
Repository
A service that stores, preserves and makes data findable, for example Zenodo or a disciplinary data archive. Search for one at re3data.org.
Pseudonymisation
Replacing direct identifiers with a code, while keeping a separate key that allows re-identification. Still personal data.
Anonymisation
Processing data so that people can no longer be identified by any reasonable means. Anonymous data fall outside the GDPR.
DPIA
Data Protection Impact Assessment: a GDPR assessment (Art. 35) of the risks of processing personal data, needed for high-risk processing such as large-scale health data.
FRDN
Flemish Research Data Network, which maintains the Flemish standard DMP template.
FWO / BOF / BELSPO
Research Foundation Flanders; the university's internal Special Research Fund; the Belgian Federal Science Policy Office.
